TWO DIFFERENT BIASES, PULLING IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS. Reading the bias
table by row rather than by column is the whole point:

  Specs A and B are not merely attenuated, they have the WRONG SIGN on
  two of three categories -- they report demand RISING with price. With no
  fixed effects, a pooled regression is identified off the price gap
  BETWEEN products, not off any product's response to its own price. It is
  comparing a $6 item to a $2 item and calling the difference elasticity.
  Adding the promo control (B) changes essentially nothing, because the
  problem was never the promo flag.

  Specs C and D add series fixed effects, which fixes the sign but leaves
  the estimate badly attenuated toward zero. That residue is a LIKELIHOOD
  problem: unit sales are counts with a large zero mass, and log1p(sales)
  is not log(sales) -- on a series that is zero most days the transform
  crushes exactly the observations carrying the price signal. No amount of
  further control fixes a misspecified link.

  Spec E (Poisson GLM, log link, but NO promo control) is biased AWAY from
  zero -- demand looks more elastic than it is. That IS the identification
  problem: prices in this panel were not randomised. The generator
  schedules promotions into weeks demand is already strong
  (src/generate.py::_price_path), and a promotion carries a display lift
  on top of its price cut. Low price and high demand therefore arrive
  together, and the regression charges the co-movement to price.

  Spec F (Poisson GLM + promo control + series FE + week-of-year) fixes
  both and lands close to truth. It is the number the markdown simulator
  uses.

The honest ceiling: spec F is still OBSERVATIONAL. It recovers truth here
because I know what confound to control for -- I wrote the generator. On
real data there is no such guarantee, and no specification search can
establish that the remaining price variation is as-good-as-random. That
takes a price experiment: randomised price cells across matched stores.
Section 9 prices exactly what not having run one costs.

BEST BY MAE: shrunk_to_category

  Per-item estimation beats the category mean, so the heterogeneity
  is not merely real but recoverable at this panel width.

  The raw per-item estimates have sd 0.8339 against a true sd of 0.7072.
  That is 1.18x the real dispersion: most of what a per-item table
  displays as 'this SKU is more elastic' is sampling noise, and a
  merchant reading it would price on it anyway.

  Shrinkage is the middle answer and it is not a compromise -- it is the
  estimator that knows how much of its own dispersion to believe. Its
  weight per item is tau^2/(tau^2+se^2) with tau^2 estimated from the
  data, so if the panel had more signal it would shrink less.

11. CROSS-PRICE ELASTICITY -- WHAT A MARKDOWN TAKES FROM THE SHELF
==============================================================================
 category                 spec      own   own_se    cross  cross_se  true_own  true_cross
    FOODS G_daily_event_dow_FE   -2.237    0.053    0.423     0.077    -2.167       0.350
HOUSEHOLD G_daily_event_dow_FE   -1.130    0.050    0.299     0.093    -1.145       0.350
  HOBBIES G_daily_event_dow_FE   -0.704    0.067    0.493     0.100    -0.604       0.350
    FOODS     H_no_calendar_FE   -2.190    0.057    0.117     0.082    -2.167       0.350
    FOODS I_rival_term_omitted   -2.181    0.052      NaN       NaN    -2.167       0.350

SPEC G recovers cross-price within 1.4 se of truth on all three
categories (planted 0.350).

SPEC H IS THE FINDING. Drop the event and day-of-week fixed effects and
cross-price collapses from 0.423 to 0.117 -- attenuated by 72%.

  It is the same confound this project already documents for own-price,
  arriving somewhere new. Promotions are scheduled into strong periods,
  and what makes a period strong here is EVENTS and WEEKDAY -- both
  shared across the whole department. So a rival's promotion lands
  disproportionately on days my demand is high for reasons that have
  nothing to do with the rival, my sales are up while the rival price is
  down, and the regression charges that co-movement to the cross term
  with a NEGATIVE sign.

  Week-of-year fixed effects do NOT fix it, which is the trap: they are
  in both specifications. Thanksgiving moves within the ISO week from
  year to year and weekday variation is inside the week by definition,
  so a weekly panel has already destroyed the variation the control
  needs. That is why this specification is daily while every other
  elasticity in this project is weekly -- aggregation is not a neutral
  performance choice when the confound lives inside the bucket.

SPEC I omits the rival term entirely -- what almost every demand model
in production does. Own-price moves +0.056 (-2.237 -> -2.181) against a true
-2.167. The own-price coefficient absorbs part of the substitution.

  Commercially the difference is between 'this markdown will sell X more
  units' and 'this markdown will sell X more units, Y of which it took
  from the item next to it'. A single-item optimiser books Y as a gain,
  and the more items in the department the more it over-discounts.

EVERY ROW IS SCORED IN THE SAME TRUE WORLD. The only thing that differs
is what the optimiser BELIEVED when it decided.

  ignores cannibalisation : depth 0.197, revenue $21313
  uses TRUE cross         : depth 0.220, revenue $21523  (+0.98%)

  Knowing about cannibalisation makes the optimiser discount MORE,
  not less. That is the opposite of the textbook direction and it is
  worth stating rather than smoothing over: with substitutes, a
  department-wide markdown partly cancels itself -- every item's
  rivals get cheaper at the same time -- so hitting the same
  clearance volume requires going DEEPER than a single-item view
  suggests. The single-item optimiser is wrong in both directions
  depending on whether it is pricing one item or a shelf.

  uses ESTIMATED cross    : depth 0.240, revenue $21619
  The estimation error costs $-96 (-0.45% of clearance revenue).
  That is the dollar value of getting the cross-price specification
  right, and it is the same argument as section 11 in a currency a
  merchant can act on.

  TRUE cross + 12% budget : depth 0.133, revenue $21500, spend $3598
  The budget costs $23 (0.11%) and is the row a merchant can actually
  approve. A schedule that ignores the season's markdown budget is not a
  recommendation, it is a wish.

  `converged` is printed because it has to be: this is a SEQUENCE of
  exact MILPs with the rival index held fixed and updated between
  solves. Convergence is tested on the schedule rather than on the
  index -- the index is damped and approaches its fixed point
  asymptotically, so testing it would raise a false alarm about a solver
  that had settled. A stable schedule on a discrete grid IS the fixed
  point, and it is the only thing a merchant ever sees.
