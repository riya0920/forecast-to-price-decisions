THE RESULT THIS SECTION EXISTS FOR, stated against my own expectation:
per-series selection LOSES to just deploying the GBM everywhere. Both
gates do. The textbook answer to 'your model loses on 18% of series' is
'select per series' -- and measured on this panel that answer is wrong.

Two mechanisms, both visible above:
  (a) WMAPE rewards a degenerate forecast on intermittent demand. On a
      mostly-zero series 'last value' is usually zero, so naive predicts
      all-zeros, scores WMAPE ~1.0, and cannot be beaten downward by any
      method that ever puts a unit on the wrong day. The plain gate duly
      hands 96 series to naive -- whose standalone bias is -29%, i.e. a
      replenishment system that under-orders every slow mover it owns.
  (b) The gate is estimated on 3 folds x 28 days of a series that sells a
      unit every third day. That is not enough signal to rank five methods,
      so the choice is mostly noise, and noise costs more than the 18% of
      series it was meant to rescue were losing.

The bias guard does what it was built to do -- it moves 54 series off naive
and pulls portfolio bias from -3.53% to -3.11% -- and still costs WMAPE.

So the deployment recommendation from this backtest is the GBM globally,
with per-series selection revisited only when there is enough evaluation
history per series to estimate the choice -- more folds, or a gate
estimated on a pooled class (intermittent/erratic/smooth) rather than on
each series alone. That pooled variant is not built here; see the README.

Reading: base forecasts are produced independently at every level and do
NOT add up -- that column is the incoherent starting point. Bottom-up is
coherent for free but discards the aggregate models entirely. The three
MinT variants differ ONLY in the weight matrix W they invert:

  mint_ols     W = I. Treats every series' error as equally important,
               which for a hierarchy spanning a 300-unit total and a
               0.2-unit item is obviously false, and it is included to
               show how false.
  mint_wls     W = diag(residual variances). Scales by each series' own
               error, which is the cheap fix and usually most of the win.
  mint_shrink  W = shrunk residual COVARIANCE. Also uses the correlation
               between series -- the thing that makes MinT better than
               scaling in principle, and the thing that is hardest to
               estimate from 353 series and 180 residual days.

Best reconciliation per level:
  total        bottom_up
  state        bottom_up
  store        bottom_up
  store_cat    bottom_up
  store_dept   bottom_up
  store_item   bottom_up

BOTTOM-UP WINS OR TIES ALMOST EVERYWHERE, and that is the honest result.
MinT should beat it in theory -- it uses information bottom-up throws
away. It does not here, and the reason is visible in the base_incoherent
column: the directly-fitted aggregate models are WORSE than aggregating
the bottom-level forecasts (total 0.11 vs 0.06), because errors cancel on
the way up. MinT blends those weaker aggregate forecasts back in, and
blending in a worse signal makes things worse. That is not a bug in MinT;
it is MinT correctly weighting information that happens not to be worth
having on this hierarchy.

==============================================================================
8. QUANTILE FORECASTS -- WHAT YOU ACTUALLY HAND REPLENISHMENT
==============================================================================
The spec's question: replenishment wants one number per item x store x
day, and the model produces a distribution. What do you hand them?

The first pass answered this in PROSE and said the code could not do it.
Now it can: a separate quantile GBM per service level, at the bottom
level, scored on the evaluation folds.

      coverage  mean_forecast  implied_cost_ratio  pinball@0.50  pinball@0.75  pinball@0.90  pinball@0.95  pinball@0.98
tau                                                                                                                    
0.50    0.6130         4.4382              1.0000        1.3516        1.5883        1.7304        1.7777        1.8061
0.75    0.7869         7.0592              3.0000        1.6526        1.2341        0.9830        0.8993        0.8490
0.90    0.9090         9.8763              9.0000        2.5804        1.4576        0.7839        0.5593        0.4246
0.95    0.9573        11.8305             19.0000        3.4018        1.7905        0.8237        0.5014        0.3080
0.98    0.9798        14.1561             49.0000        4.4710        2.2783        0.9627        0.5241        0.2610

COVERAGE is the calibration check: the share of actual days at or below
the forecast. For a well-calibrated tau-quantile it should equal tau.
  tau=0.50  target coverage 0.50  achieved 0.6130  (+0.1130)
  tau=0.75  target coverage 0.75  achieved 0.7869  (+0.0369)
  tau=0.90  target coverage 0.90  achieved 0.9090  (+0.0090)
  tau=0.95  target coverage 0.95  achieved 0.9573  (+0.0073)
  tau=0.98  target coverage 0.98  achieved 0.9798  (-0.0002)

THE P50 OVER-COVERS BADLY (0.6130 against a target of 0.50) and the reason
is worth stating rather than glossing: coverage counts actual <= forecast,
and on a series that is zero 40%% of the time the median forecast is often
ZERO. Every zero day then counts as covered, because 0 <= 0. That is a
property of coverage as a diagnostic on discrete zero-inflated data, not
a miscalibrated model -- and the pinball diagonal below confirms the P50
is in fact the best P50 available. It is a reminder that a calibration
check needs to be read against the data it is computed on: the same
statistic that is informative at tau=0.95 is nearly meaningless at 0.50
here.

THE DIAGONAL IS THE PROOF. Each row is scored on EVERY tau's pinball
loss, and a calibrated set of quantiles should have each forecast
winning the loss for the tau it was fitted to:
  pinball@0.50 is minimised by tau=0.50  OK
  pinball@0.75 is minimised by tau=0.75  OK
  pinball@0.90 is minimised by tau=0.90  OK
  pinball@0.95 is minimised by tau=0.95  OK
  pinball@0.98 is minimised by tau=0.98  OK

5 of 5 quantiles win their own loss.

WHY PINBALL AND NOT MAE: MAE is minimised by the MEDIAN, so scoring a
P90 on MAE would rank it worse than the P50 by construction -- it would
be measuring the wrong thing and calling the right answer wrong. The
pinball loss is the proper scoring rule for a quantile: at tau=0.9 an
under-forecast is penalised 9x more than an over-forecast of the same
size, which is exactly the asymmetry a 90% service level asserts.

SO WHAT DO YOU HAND THEM. Not the mean. Ordering to the mean means being
short about half the time, and the two errors do not cost the same. You
hand them the quantile implied by the item's service target, and the
newsvendor result says which one:

    q* = Cu / (Cu + Co)

where Cu is the cost of being one unit short and Co of one too many.
Read the implied_cost_ratio column backwards and a service level stops
being a policy and becomes a claim somebody has to defend:
  a 90% service target ASSERTS that understocking costs 9x
  what overstocking costs.
  a 95% service target ASSERTS that understocking costs 19x
  what overstocking costs.
  a 98% service target ASSERTS that understocking costs 49x
  what overstocking costs.

The mean forecast sits at 5.4156 units/day and the P95 at 11.8305 -- the gap
is the safety stock the service level is buying, and it is 118% more
inventory than ordering to the mean would hold.

HONEST LIMITS. These are DAILY quantiles, and replenishment needs the
quantile of demand over the LEAD TIME, which is not the sum of daily
quantiles -- summing quantiles overstates, because the days do not all
go wrong together. Converting one to the other needs the dependence
structure across days, which is exactly what data2's negative-binomial
experiment found it was missing. Neither project has it, and they
independently arrived at the same gap, which is at least consistent.
